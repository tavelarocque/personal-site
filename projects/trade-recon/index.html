trade-recon

Every trade is recorded twice, once by the firm and once by its broker. trade-recon finds where the two records disagree before settlement, names each disagreement, and ranks them by the cash at stake.

The problem

When a firm buys shares through a broker, both sides write the trade down. The firm's list is its blotter, and the broker sends back a statement. The two should agree on every trade: what was bought, how many, at what price, in which direction, and when the cash moves. A disagreement is called a break.

Breaks have to be found and fixed before the trade settles, which is one business day after the trade in the US and two in Hong Kong. A break found after that is cash or shares in the wrong place.

How it works

  1. 01
    Generate. A seeded blotter of 500 US and Hong Kong equity trades, and the broker's version of it: different column names, different codes and date formats, and a set of planted breaks logged in an answer key.
  2. 02
    Normalize. The broker file is read as text, cleaned, and only then converted. An unknown side code or an ambiguous date stops the run.
  3. 03
    Match. Trades are paired by ID. Leftovers are paired by symbol, side, quantity and trade date. If two candidates fit, it pairs neither: the fallback refuses to guess.
  4. 04
    Classify and size. Each break gets a type and two numbers: a signed cash difference in local currency, and an absolute impact in USD for ranking.
  5. 05
    Settlement. NYSE and HKEX calendars for 2026 and 2027, including the days that trade but do not settle, give each trade its expected settlement date and each break its urgency.
  6. 06
    Report. Breaks are ranked by impact, each with a one-line suggested action, and summarised by type, broker and urgency.

Three checks, and why one is not enough

Against the answer key. A found break counts only if the trade, the type and the cash to the cent all match what was planted.

Totals. The firm's total cash minus the broker's total cash must equal the sum of the breaks, in each currency. This is computed from the raw books and does not depend on the matching. It proves no cash is unaccounted for. It cannot prove each break is right, because errors that cancel pass straight through it: a trade wrongly reported as missing on one side and unrecognised on the other adds to zero.

Against the outside world. Settlement dates are tested against the exchanges' published calendars, and the cash convention against numbers worked out by hand. Twelve bugs are then planted in the engine one at a time, and the test suite has to catch every one.

What went wrong: Veterans Day

The first version computed settlement dates by skipping weekends and market holidays, and every check passed. It was still wrong.

Wednesday 11 November 2026 is Veterans Day. The New York Stock Exchange trades normally, but US banks are closed, so nothing settles. A US trade from Tuesday the 10th settles on Thursday the 12th, not on Wednesday. The first version settled 23 trades on a day when nothing can settle.

No test caught it, because the tests shared the code's assumption: that a day the market is open is a day trades can settle. Those are two separate questions, and a calendar that answers only the first is wrong a few days every year. The error surfaced only on reading the exchange and clearing-house schedules directly.

The calendar now keeps two lists per market: days the market is closed, and days it trades but does not settle. One of the twelve planted bugs removes Veterans Day from the second list, to prove the tests now see it. A check only protects against the mistakes it was designed to see.

Limits

  • The data is synthetic. Real broker files are messier in ways not planted here.
  • Each trade carries at most one planted break.
  • Calendars cover 2026 and 2027 only. Other dates raise an error.
  • One fixed FX rate is used for ranking.
